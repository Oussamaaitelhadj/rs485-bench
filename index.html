Experiment 1

Two wires, one difference

Each bit is sent on two wires driven in opposite directions. The receiver ignores each wire's voltage on its own and only looks at A − B. Above +200 mV it reads 1; below −200 mV it reads 0.

Interference on a twisted pair hits both wires almost equally. That shared shift is common-mode noise, and it disappears in the subtraction. A single-ended receiver, which compares one wire to ground like RS232 does, has no way to remove it.

Try this

  • Push common-mode noise to 7 V. The wires swing wildly, the differential receiver still reads every bit.
  • Press Untwisted wire. Noise that hits one wire more than the other is what really breaks RS485.
  • Lower the signal swing until errors appear. That is the noise margin between the driver's 1.5 V and the receiver's 0.2 V.

Experiment 2

The driver is four switches

The driver doesn't choose a voltage. It's an H-bridge: to send 1 it connects A toward the supply and B toward ground, and to send 0 it swaps them. A 0 isn't a negative voltage on a wire. It's the same two voltages, swapped.

The levels you measure come from the supply, the transistor drops, and the load. The standard only requires at least 1.5 V of difference into 54 Ω, which is two 120 Ω terminators plus 32 receivers.

Try this

  • Toggle the bit and watch which switches close and which way current flows through the load.
  • Change the load from open to 54 Ω. The swing shrinks because the driver has internal resistance.
  • Switch to 3.3 V. It still passes the 1.5 V spec, with less margin.

Experiment 3

Ground offset doesn't flip bits

Over long runs, the sender's ground and the receiver's ground drift apart. From the receiver's side, that offset is added to both wires, so it cancels when you subtract: (A + offset) − (B + offset) = A − B. The sign of the bit survives in either direction.

The limit is the receiver's input window, −7 V to +12 V relative to its own ground. Outside it, the input circuitry can't subtract accurately and protection diodes start to conduct. That's why installations still run a ground reference wire or use isolated transceivers.

Try this

  • Drag the offset both ways. A − B keeps the same value and sign on both sides.
  • Push the offset past +9 V or below −8 V and watch a wire leave the green window.

Experiment 4

Termination and reflections

A twisted pair behaves like a transmission line with a characteristic impedance of about 120 Ω. An edge travels down it at roughly 5 ns per metre. If the far end isn't terminated with 120 Ω, part of the edge reflects back, hits the low-impedance driver, reflects again, and rings.

Ringing only causes errors if it hasn't died out by the time the receiver samples the bit, near the middle. That depends on cable length, edge speed and bit time, which is why slow, short buses often work unterminated.

Try this

  • Open far end, 100 m, fast edges: look at the overshoot and ringing in the zoomed edge.
  • Keep it open but drop to 9600 baud. The ringing is still there, it just ends long before the sample point.
  • Go to 1 Mbps with 300 m unterminated, then add 120 Ω.
  • Try slew-limited edges on an open line: slow edges barely ring on short cables.

Experiment 5

Bias and the idle bus

Between messages no driver is on. The terminators tie A and B together, so A − B sits near 0 V. That isn't safely below a threshold: a real receiver switches somewhere near 0 V, and ±200 mV is only the worst-case guarantee. A few millivolts of differential noise flips the output, and each dip to 0 looks like a UART start bit.

Bias is a pull-up on A and a pull-down on B, placed once on the bus. With the terminators it forms a voltage divider that holds the idle bus at about +250 mV, beyond every receiver's threshold.

Try this

  • Turn bias off. Garbage bytes appear in the long gap between the two real messages.
  • Turn it back on and raise the noise. The idle level now has margin.
  • Pick 4.7 kΩ bias with two terminators. Too weak to beat 60 Ω, so the margin is gone.
  • Switch to a true fail-safe receiver with bias off. Its threshold is shifted negative, so 0 V already reads as 1.

Experiment 6

How a UART frames a byte

The idle line is 1. A byte begins with a start bit (0), then 8 data bits, least significant first, then a stop bit (1). The receiver waits for the falling edge, checks half a bit later that the line is still 0, then samples each bit in its middle.

There is no clock wire, so both sides must agree on the baud rate. A small clock error is absorbed because the receiver re-synchronizes on every start bit, but the error builds up over the 10 bits of the frame.

Try this

  • Type a character and press Sample to watch the receiver take its samples one by one.
  • Slide the receiver clock error to ±4 %, then ±6 %. The last samples drift into the wrong bit.

Experiment 7

Many devices, one pair

Every receiver on the bus hears every byte. Its UART decodes them all in hardware, and the firmware reads the first byte, the address, to decide whether the frame is meant for it. A slave that isn't addressed ignores the content but keeps listening, because it still has to find where the frame ends so it can catch the start of the next one. Modbus RTU marks the end with a silence of 3.5 character times.

Only one node may drive at a time. A node turns its driver on (DE), sends, and must wait until the last stop bit has fully left before releasing the bus.

Try this

  • Send a read to slave 2 and watch slaves 1 and 3 receive every byte but ignore it.
  • Send to address 0. It's a broadcast: every slave acts on it and nobody replies.
  • Tick Release DE too early. The reply's last byte is cut off and the master's CRC check fails.
